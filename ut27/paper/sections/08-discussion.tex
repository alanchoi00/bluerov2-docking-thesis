\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the oracle and the rescaled arm decided.} \Bgt{} feeds the true dock velocity through the same law as arm B, so it isolates the plant from the estimator. It over-swings as B does at every period, which places the over-swing in the open-loop law and the plant. It also seats at 8~s where B does not, so at that one period the estimate's phase lag, through a lag-limited plant, decides whether the small markers stay in view. Both repairs remove the over-swing at every period with the same estimate. The rescaled open-loop arm settles the second question the oracle leaves open: it docks without contact at every period including 6~s with only the gain changed, so under exact navigation the fault was the gain, and the open-loop structure was sound once the plant was identified. The ablation adds the lesson for anyone who closes the loop instead: every gain, limit and ramp downstream of the controller output was tuned as an effort, each one left unconverted cost a measured failure, and the pure PI at full speed needed the model feedforward to keep its lag down (9 of 18 under bias without it).

\textbf{Transfer to hardware.} The cliff sits where the position loop's return ratio at the sway frequency falls toward unity; a longer plant lag lowers it further. A real vehicle adds thruster response, autopilot filtering and tether drag, so the lag will grow; the gain must be re-identified, and the rescaled law stands or falls with that number. The velocity-closed feedforward needs only the identified lag to place its bandwidth, and a body-velocity measurement to close on. A stock BlueROV2 has none; the arm assumes a Doppler velocity log, whose bias and noise the navigation-error levels bracket. The loop closes here on a 100~Hz velocity; a Doppler log at 5 to 15~Hz with bottom-track latency adds a lag the sweep does not model, which the 2~rad/s bandwidth tolerates only while that lag stays well under the 0.4~s plant time constant, and a Doppler log within a metre of a moving dock face may track the dock rather than the seabed. Without a velocity sensor only the rescaled law is available.

\textbf{Navigation error.} The observability limit of Section~\ref{sec:navobs} has a plain practical reading, and the live sweep shows it: an open-loop feedforward on a real navigation solution drives the vehicle at the dock velocity plus $K_{ff}\,g$ times the bias. The original method's collapse at a 3~cm/s drift was the same gain error once more, amplifying the bias 2.7 times; with the gain corrected the open loop carries the bias at one times and the position feedback absorbs it, 16 of 18 trials, leaving a visible offset at the entry plane. Closing the feedforward on the same navigation solution cancels the bias in the loop error, 16 of 18 with millimetre offsets. What the velocity loop buys for a deployment is therefore not a margin in docking count at these levels but, by the argument of Section~\ref{sec:redesign} and not by a test in this sweep, tolerance of an error in $g$, which changes with flight mode, trim and hardware; cancellation of a bias, to within the estimator's tracking lag, that the position loop would otherwise hold as a steady offset of $b_v / (K_p g)$ with the position gain, 4.6~cm at a steady 10~cm/s with the fine gains; and the most accurate insertion in this sweep, at the rescaled law's speed. The order of importance for a deployment is therefore the plant identification, then the bounded velocity state, then the choice of law by how well $g$ is known, and the choice of estimator last. The reactive baseline is the arm that navigation drift hurts most here, because its constant-position filter lives in the world frame through the vehicle pose and has nothing to absorb the drift with. The sensitivity belongs to the world-frame formulation; a reactive servo on the camera-frame pose alone, as Trsli\'c et al.\ ran it \cite{trslic2020vision}, never sees the navigation, at the cost of filtering across dropouts.

\textbf{Where the fix stops.} At a 10~cm/s drift the world-frame filter saturates its 0.2~m/s velocity bound and the estimate walks away from the dock with the markers in view; no controller recovers that. Arm C loses two trials of six there, the rescaled arm one there and one at the medium level to a marker loss. The bound was sized for the dock's own motion and kept fixed across arms; sizing it with the expected drift, or estimating the drift as a state, is the next step.

\textbf{Limitations.} Contact is not modelled; success is scored at the capture envelope and would-be contacts are censored. The dock motion is a single prescribed sinusoid at one amplitude in still water, so what is tested is a 0.1~m relative motion. The orbital flow that moves a real dock also advects the vehicle, a moored dock yaws and pitches, and there is no current, tether or surge; a multi-sine, a yawing dock and a flow field on the vehicle are the next cells. At the 0.55~m cage amplitude of Trsli\'c et al.\ the dock velocity exceeds the 0.2~m/s bound and the 0.25~m/s clamp, which must scale with the site. Perception is rendered without water, so the 0.2 to 0.9~cm measurement error is a property of the renderer; real ArUco poses jitter at the centimetre level, which a constant-velocity filter differentiates into velocity, so a replay with measured marker jitter is the next test of the estimator. The plant gain and time constant come from open-loop step tests in the simulator and arm C's velocity loop is tuned against them, so the same tests must be repeated on hardware before the gains transfer.
