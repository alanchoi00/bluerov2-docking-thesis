\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the oracle and the rescaled arm decided.} \Bgt{} feeds the true dock velocity through the same law as arm B, so it isolates the plant from the estimator. It over-swings as B does at every period, which places the over-swing in the open-loop law and the plant. It also seats at 8~s where B does not, in two of three trials without contact, so at that one period the estimate's phase decides the outcome on top of the over-swing: the filter's velocity is accurate in amplitude but lags, and the lagged feedforward through a lag-limited plant carries the small markers out of view. Both repairs remove the over-swing at every period with the same estimate. The rescaled open-loop arm settles the second question the oracle leaves open: it docks without contact at every period including 6~s with only the gain changed, so under exact navigation the fault was the gain, and the open-loop structure was sound once the plant was identified. The ablation adds the lesson for anyone who closes the loop instead: every gain, limit and ramp downstream of the controller output was tuned as an effort, each one left unconverted cost a measured failure, and the pure PI at full speed needed the model feedforward to stay stable.

\textbf{Transfer to hardware.} The cliff sits where the plant lag becomes comparable to the sway period. The autopilot is the production firmware; the rest of the lag is the vehicle's mass and added mass under bounded thrust. A real vehicle adds thruster response, autopilot filtering and tether drag, so the lag will grow; the gain must be re-identified, and the rescaled law stands or falls with that number. The velocity-closed feedforward needs only the identified lag to place its bandwidth, and a body-velocity measurement to close on. A stock BlueROV2 has none; the arm assumes a Doppler velocity log, whose bias and noise the navigation-error levels bracket, and a Doppler log within a metre of a moving dock face may track the dock rather than the seabed in the final metre, which the sweep does not model.

\textbf{Navigation error.} The observability limit of Section~\ref{sec:navobs} has a plain practical reading, and the live sweep shows it. Any onboard dock-velocity estimate carries the vehicle's own slow velocity error, whatever frame it is estimated in, so an open-loop feedforward on a real navigation solution drives the vehicle at the dock velocity plus $K_{ff}\,g$ times the bias. The original method's collapse at a 3~cm/s drift was the same gain error once more, amplifying the bias 2.7 times; with the gain corrected the open loop carries the bias at one times and the position feedback absorbs it, 16 of 18 trials, leaving a visible offset at the entry plane. Closing the feedforward on the same navigation solution cancels the bias in the loop error, 16 of 18 with millimetre offsets. What the velocity loop buys for a deployment is therefore not a margin in docking count at these levels but independence from $g$, which changes with flight mode, trim and hardware, exact cancellation of a bias the position loop would otherwise hold as a steady offset of $b_v / (K_p g)$, 4.6~cm at 10~cm/s with the fine gains, and the most accurate and fastest insertion of any arm. The order of importance for a deployment is therefore the plant identification, then the bounded velocity state, then the choice of law by how well $g$ is known, and the choice of estimator last. The reactive baseline is the arm that navigation drift hurts most here, because its constant-position filter lives in the world frame through the vehicle pose and has nothing to absorb the drift with. The sensitivity belongs to the world-frame formulation and would not appear in a reactive servo on the camera-frame pose alone, which, as Trsli\'c et al.\ ran it \cite{trslic2020vision}, never sees the navigation, at the cost of the filtering across dropouts that the world frame allows.

\textbf{Where the fix stops.} At a 10~cm/s drift the world-frame filter saturates its 0.2~m/s velocity bound and the estimate walks away from the dock with the markers still in view; no controller recovers that, and both repairs lose two trials of six there. The bound was set for the reactive baseline on a fixed dock and kept fixed across arms; sizing it with the expected drift, or estimating the drift as its own state, is the next step.

\textbf{Limitations.} Contact is not modelled; success is scored at the capture envelope and would-be structural contacts are censored, which the simulation cannot show. The dock motion is a single prescribed sinusoid at one amplitude in still water, so what is tested is a 0.1~m relative motion. The orbital flow that moves a real dock also advects the vehicle, a moored dock yaws and pitches, and there is no current, tether or surge; a multi-sine, a yawing dock and a flow field on the vehicle are the next cells. At the 0.55~m cage amplitude of Trsli\'c et al.\ the peak dock velocity of 0.4~m/s exceeds the 0.2~m/s velocity bound and the 0.25~m/s clamp, which must scale with the site. Perception is rendered without water, so the 0.2 to 0.9~cm measurement error is a property of the renderer; real ArUco poses at 2 to 4~m jitter at the centimetre level, which a constant-velocity filter differentiates into its velocity state, so a replay with measured marker jitter is the next test of the estimator half of the fix. The plant gain and time constant come from open-loop step tests in the simulator and arm C's velocity loop is tuned against them, so the same tests must be repeated on hardware before the gains transfer. The second host's velocity-state amplitudes are biased low by a wall-clock filter defect fixed before the comparison.
