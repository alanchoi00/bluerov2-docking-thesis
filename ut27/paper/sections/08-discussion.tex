\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the oracle decided.} Arm D feeds the true dock velocity through the same law as arm B, so it isolates the plant from the estimator. It over-swings as B does at every period, which places the over-swing in the open-loop law and the plant, and it fails at 6~s where no open-loop arm latches (its two 6~s contacts would count as captures on a funnel dock). It also seats at 8~s where B does not, in two of three trials without contact, so at that one period the estimate's phase decides the outcome on top of the over-swing: the constant-velocity filter's velocity is accurate in amplitude but lags, and the lagged feedforward through a lag-limited plant is what carries the small markers out of view. The velocity-closed arm removes the over-swing at every period with the same estimate, and is the only arm without a contact down to 8~s, so the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:failure} and the velocity-closed feedforward of Section~\ref{sec:redesign} are both confirmed.

\textbf{Transfer to hardware.} The cliff sits where the plant lag becomes comparable to the sway period. The autopilot is the production firmware, and the rest of the lag is the vehicle's mass and added mass under bounded thrust in the simulator's hydrodynamic model. A real vehicle adds thruster response, the autopilot's own filtering and tether drag, so the lag is likely to grow and we expect an open-loop feedforward to fail at the same or longer periods; the gain must be re-identified, since the sign of its mismatch on hardware is unknown. The velocity-closed feedforward needs only the identified lag to place its bandwidth, and a body-velocity measurement to close on. A stock BlueROV2 has none; the arm assumes a Doppler velocity log, whose bias and noise the navigation-error levels bracket, and a Doppler log within a metre of a moving dock face may track the dock rather than the seabed in the final metre, which the sweep does not model.

\textbf{Navigation error.} The observability limit of Section~\ref{sec:navobs} has a plain practical reading, and the live sweep shows it. Any onboard dock-velocity estimate carries the vehicle's own slow velocity error, whatever frame it is estimated in, so an open-loop feedforward on a real navigation solution drives the vehicle at the dock velocity plus the bias, which is what removed the original method from the dock at a 3~cm/s drift. Closing the feedforward on the same navigation solution cancels the bias, 17 of 18 trials against 4 of 18, which makes the velocity loop of Eq.~\eqref{eq:vloop} the part of the fix that matters most for a deployment, ahead of the choice of estimator. The reactive baseline is the arm that navigation drift hurts most here, because its constant-position filter lives in the world frame through the vehicle pose and has nothing to absorb the drift with. That is a property of the world-frame formulation, not of reactive docking: a servo on the camera-frame pose alone, as Trsli\'c et al.\ ran \cite{trslic2020vision}, never sees the navigation and is immune to its drift, at the cost of the filtering across dropouts that the world frame allows.

\textbf{The reactive baseline.} It docks at every period, including 6~s, and it is the fastest to seat. It is also the arm with the largest entry-plane offsets from 8~s down and the one that contacts the aperture most often there: it does not track the dock, it rides the swing and latches when the phase is favourable, which a funnel dock would forgive.

\textbf{Where the fix stops.} Two limits were reached. At 6~s the coarse handoff gate reads the velocity loop's own phase lag, 28$^\circ$ by design, as relative motion and rarely latches, so the arm holds the dock to 3~cm and waits; a lead term or a higher inner bandwidth would move it. At a 10~cm/s navigation drift the world-frame filter must carry sway plus drift above its 0.2~m/s velocity bound, the estimate the setpoint is built on lags, and one trial in six is lost at 10~s. Both are margins set for the reactive baseline on a fixed dock and kept fixed across arms.

\textbf{Limitations.} Contact is not modelled; success is scored at the capture envelope and would-be structural contacts are censored, which the simulation cannot show. The dock motion is a single prescribed sinusoid at one amplitude in still water, so what is tested is a 0.1~m relative motion: the orbital flow that moves a real dock also advects the vehicle, a moored dock yaws and pitches, and there is no current, tether or surge; a multi-sine, a yawing dock and a flow field on the vehicle are the next cells. At the amplitude of the cage of Trsli\'c et al.\ (0.55~m at 8.5~s) the peak dock velocity of 0.4~m/s exceeds both the 0.2~m/s velocity-state bound and the 0.25~m/s feedforward clamp, so the bounds scale with the amplitude expected at a site. Perception is rendered without water: the imagery is noise-free, evenly lit and unattenuated, and the 0.2 to 0.9~cm measurement error is a property of the renderer. Real ArUco poses at 2 to 4~m jitter at the centimetre level, which a constant-velocity filter differentiates into its velocity state, so the ordering of the two halves of the fix may not survive real imagery; a replay with measured marker jitter is the test. The nominal sweep uses ground-truth navigation, which is what made the mechanism identifiable and is also why the navigation-error sweep is reported separately. The plant gain and time constant come from open-loop step tests in the simulator and arm C's velocity loop is tuned against them, so the same tests must be repeated on hardware before the gains transfer. The rescaled open-loop law was not run. Before the sim-time fix of the filter node, the filter stepped on wall-clock time and its velocity state scaled with the real-time factor; the first host ran near real time and is little affected, whereas the second ran at 0.6 and its velocity-state amplitudes are biased low.
