\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the oracle and the rescaled arm decided.} \Bgt{} feeds the true dock velocity through arm B's law and over-swings as B does at every period, which places the over-swing in the open-loop law and the plant. It also seats at 8~s where B does not, so at that one period the estimate's phase lag decides whether the small markers stay in view. The rescaled arm settles the question the oracle leaves open: it docks without contact at every period with only the gain changed, so under exact navigation the fault was the gain, and the open-loop structure was sound once the plant was identified. The ablation adds the lesson for anyone who closes the loop instead: each gain, limit and ramp left in effort units cost a measured failure, and the pure PI at full speed needed the model feedforward to latch every core trial.

\textbf{Transfer to hardware.} The cliff sits where the position loop's return ratio at the sway frequency falls toward unity; a longer plant lag lowers it further. A real vehicle adds thruster response, autopilot filtering and tether drag, so the lag will grow; the gain must be re-identified, and the rescaled law stands or falls with that number. The velocity-closed feedforward needs only the identified lag to place its bandwidth, and a body-velocity measurement to close on. A stock BlueROV2 has none; the arm assumes a Doppler velocity log. Held pings at the largest measured noise cost it about 5~s of seat time at 10~Hz and no resolvable docks; at 4~Hz the hold alone adds 14~s and the same noise a further 16~s, all of it waiting at the handoff, so a slow log needs the loop bandwidth lowered, a model prediction between pings or a handoff limit sized to the hold, none tested here. Bottom-track latency is not modelled, and a Doppler log within a metre of a moving dock face may track the dock rather than the seabed. Without a velocity sensor only the rescaled law applies.

\textbf{Navigation error.} The live sweep shows the observability limit of Section~\ref{sec:navobs} in practice: an open-loop feedforward on a real navigation solution drives the vehicle at the dock velocity plus $K_{ff}\,g$ times the bias. The original design's collapse at a 3~cm/s drift was the same gain error once more; with the gain corrected the open loop carries the bias once and the position feedback absorbs it, leaving a visible offset at the entry plane, and the velocity loop cancels it in the loop error. What the velocity loop buys for a deployment is therefore not a margin in docking count at these levels but, by the argument of Section~\ref{sec:redesign} and not by a test in this sweep, tolerance of an error in $g$, which changes with flight mode, trim and hardware; cancellation of a bias that the position loop would otherwise hold as a steady entry offset; and the most accurate insertion in this sweep, at the rescaled law's speed. The reactive baseline's sensitivity to drift belongs to its world-frame filter; a reactive servo on the camera-frame pose alone, as Trsli\'c et al.\ ran it \cite{trslic2020vision}, never sees the navigation, at the cost of filtering across dropouts.

\textbf{Where the fix stops.} At a 10~cm/s drift the world-frame filter saturates its 0.2~m/s velocity bound and the estimate walks away from the dock with the markers in view; no controller recovers that, and sizing the bound with the expected drift is the next step.

\textbf{Limitations.} Contact is not modelled; success is scored at the capture envelope and would-be contacts are censored. The dock motion is a single prescribed sinusoid at one amplitude in still water, so what is tested is a 0.1~m relative motion. The orbital flow that moves a real dock also advects the vehicle, a moored dock yaws and pitches, and there is no current, tether or surge. At the 0.55~m cage amplitude of Trsli\'c et al.\ the dock velocity exceeds the 0.2~m/s bound and the 0.25~m/s clamp, which must scale with the site. Perception is rendered without water, so the 0.2 to 0.9~cm measurement error is a property of the renderer; real ArUco poses jitter at the centimetre level, which a constant-velocity filter differentiates into velocity, so a replay with measured marker jitter is the next test of the estimator.
